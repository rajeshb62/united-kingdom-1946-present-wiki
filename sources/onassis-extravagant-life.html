Onassis: An Extravagant Life (Brady, 1977)

A single-volume popular biography of the Greek shipowner Aristotle Socrates Onassis (1906–1975), told in fourteen untitled chapters that open on set-piece scenes and move between his business career (tobacco, tankers, surplus ships, Saudi Arabia, Germany, Monaco, whaling, Olympic Airways) and his private life (Ingebord Dedichen, Tina Livanos, Maria Callas, Jacqueline Kennedy), ending with the death of his son Alexander and his own death.

Summary

Frank Brady’s Onassis: An Extravagant Life (Prentice-Hall, 1977) follows Onassis from the Greek quarter of Smyrna, where he was born on 20 January 1906 into a prosperous tobacco-exporting family, to his death in the American Hospital in Paris on 15 March 1975. The early chapters cover the family in Smyrna, his mother’s death, the Turkish capture and burning of the city in September 1922, his father’s imprisonment and ransom, the loss of the family’s fortune, and his departure at seventeen for Buenos Aires with about sixty dollars. In Argentina he works for the telephone company, builds a Turkish-tobacco import business, is sent to Athens in 1929 over a Greece–Argentina tariff dispute and returns as Greek Consul General.

The middle chapters are the business narrative. Onassis buys six Canadian freighters in Depression-era Montreal at about $20,000 each, registers ships under flags of convenience (Panama, later Liberia), orders the 15,000-ton tanker Ariston at Götaverken on Scandinavian credit terms (launched June 1938), sits out the early war with his Swedish-held ships idle, and is watched by the F.B.I. in New York. After 1945 he marries Tina Livanos, daughter of the shipowner Stavros Livanos (the book has the marriage under a month old in early 1947), begins the lifelong rivalry with his brother-in-law Stavros Niarchos, acquires U.S. surplus tankers and dry-cargo ships through a nominally American company, and is indicted in 1953–54 in the surplus-ships prosecution (settled December 1955). He signs a tanker monopoly with King Saud (January 1954), loses it after ARAMCO and the oil majors retaliate, and is rescued by the closing of the Suez Canal in 1956. In parallel he buys control of the Société des Bains de Mer in Monte Carlo (January 1953), runs a Panamanian-flag whaling fleet in the Antarctic and off Peru (1950–55), builds the yacht Christina, takes over Greece’s national airline as Olympic Airways, and becomes a close companion of Winston Churchill.

The final third is personal: the Callas affair and Tina’s divorce, the courtship of Jacqueline Kennedy after 1963, the October 1968 wedding on Skorpios, the failed $400 million Greek junta investment package, the strain in the marriage, the 1973 oil-tanker miscalculation, Alexander’s fatal air crash, Onassis’s myasthenia gravis and death. A short Afterword lists his contradictions. The book is a narrative biography for a general reader: scenes are reconstructed, dialogue is given, and many episodes rest on interviews and on earlier writers (Willi Frischauer, Fred Sparks, Joachim Joesten, Doris Lilly, Peter Hawkins and others) whom Brady quotes by name. Where he cannot verify a claim he sometimes says so (“pure speculation,” “it was rumored,” “there appears to have been conflicting reports”), and the reader should keep that distinction.

Contents

The book has no table of contents and chapter numbers are not printed on every opener in the scan; the fourteen chapters below are identified from the opening vignettes and page breaks, and the Afterword closes the main text.

Chapter 1 — Smyrna childhood (pp. 1–12)

Opens on a six-year-old Aristo being bathed by his grandmother Gethsemane in the family villa at Karatas above the Gulf of Smyrna. Brady then goes back to about 1901, when Socrates Onassis, a Turkish citizen of Greek allegiance from Kayseri, moves to Smyrna, apprentices with the Jewish merchant Bohor Benadava, marries Penelope Dologlu and builds a tobacco-export firm that becomes the third largest business in the city, with credit at the Imperial Ottoman Bank. Aristotle is born on 20 January 1906; a sister, Artemis, is two years older. The Greek minority’s position under the sultans, the Zeybekiko dance, and the household’s Greek and Turkish mix are sketched.

His mother dies of uremic poisoning at 25 after a kidney operation at the French Hospital; the funeral and the party afterward confuse the child. His father remarries (Helen), and half-sisters Merope and Calirrhoe follow. Aristo attends private schools and the Evangeliki Scholi, works as his father’s messenger boy, learns the tobacco trade, smokes cigars at twelve, and is expected to go on to Oxford. His grandmother hoped he would become a priest. The German general Liman von Sanders’s occupation, the fez, the 1919 Greek landing and Socrates’s role as treasurer of the Greek-support organisation “Ameni” set the political backdrop. The chapter closes on his early sexual experiences (a laundress at eleven, the French tutor), reported on the later authority of Ingebord Dedichen, and on his father’s advice: “Sleep up.”

Chapter 2 — The burning of Smyrna and departure for Argentina (pp. 13–24)

Opens on the burning city in September 1922: refugees on the quays, ships in the harbor, Aristotle moving through the streets at night. Brady supplies the background (Greece divided in the First World War, Venizelos and the 1919–22 Greco-Turkish War, Kemal’s counter-offensive, the Greek retreat and its reprisals) and describes the Turkish occupation of 9 September, the killings, the fire, and the later removal of some 1,500,000 Greeks from Turkey. Socrates is arrested and held in the Cunak prison; an uncle is hanged, other relatives are interned or burned in a church; the women and girls are sent to evacuation camps and Aristotle, nearly seventeen and fluent in Turkish, is allowed to stay in the requisitioned villa.

Through a laisser-passer and a pass to the U.S. Marine Zone from Vice-Consul John L. Parker, he visits his father, recovers a few thousand Turkish pounds from a safe in the burnt office, organises about fifty Turkish business friends to picket the prison, and bribes guards for better treatment. He escapes the warden and is taken to Greece on an American destroyer. In Athens the family of almost two dozen lives on about 100,000;hisgrandmotherdiesafteranattackinPiraeus.AristotlereturnsbywayofConstantinople,whereroughly25,000 in bribes secures Socrates’s pardon; the father then criticises the expense as wasteful, a rift that Brady says lasted the rest of Onassis’s life. With no money for Oxford and no work in a country absorbing 1,500,000 refugees, he borrows a ticket and leaves Piraeus on 3 August 1923, not yet eighteen.

Chapter 3 — The crossing and Buenos Aires (pp. 25–32)

Opens aboard the Tommaso di Savoia in steerage, from which Onassis buys his way onto the deck. He learns Italian on the trip through Brindisi, Naples and the Atlantic crossing, lands at Buenos Aires on 21 September 1923 with about sixty dollars and a Nansen passport, and applies for Argentine citizenship. Brady stresses that he began “Horatio Alger-like” without his father’s money. After casual work (dishwashing, bricklaying, rowing) he is hired by the British United River Plate Telephone Company (an I.T.T. affiliate), becomes a night switchboard operator, perfects his Spanish and picks up English and German, and saves close to $1,000 in about six months.

He then goes into Oriental-tobacco importing with samples from his father. After weeks of failing to see a buyer he catches the president of a cigarette manufacturer, Juan Gaona, on the street, and takes a 10,000orderatadeliberatelylowprice.WithintwoyearsheraisesOrientalleafto35percentoftobaccousedinBuenosAires(about2 million of imports, personal profit over $100,000). The chapter breaks off at a cousin’s name at the plate insert.

Chapter 4 — Envoy, Consul General and the first ships (pp. 37–47)

Opens on Onassis waiting in the Athens Foreign Ministry in 1929 for Minister Andreas Michalakopoulos. In summer 1929, after six years away and with his father ill, he returns as a millionaire; the Greek–Argentine tariff retaliation threatens his tobacco business and the Greek merchant fleet. Venizelos receives him as a favor to Socrates; the minister files his nails until Onassis walks out, and then gives him diplomatic status as Envoy Extraordinary to negotiate a trade agreement. In December 1929 he is named Greek Consul General in Buenos Aires, a post he treats as a shipping “listening post.” He meets Costa Gratsos, who teaches him ship operation.

He quotes himself: “My favorite country is the one that grants maximum immunity from taxes, trade restrictions and unreasonable regulations.” Looking ahead to oil and motors, he concludes supertankers will be the future.

Chapter 5 — Ingebord Dedichen, the Ariston and the Second World War (pp. 48–64)

Opens aboard the Augustus in 1934 as Onassis tries to persuade the Norwegian Ingebord Dedichen (then 35, divorcing the bridge player Herman Dedichen and travelling with the whaling owner Lars Christensen) to go to Venice. They do, and their relationship lasts more than a dozen years; he sets up a fourteen-room apartment at 88 Avenue Foch, Paris, and she tutors him in manners, wine, restaurants and art. Through her contacts he gets to Ernst Heden at Götaverken and places an order for the 15,000-ton Ariston, financed with 10 percent down and the balance over ten years; two more tankers follow, the Aristophanes and the Buenos Aires. He becomes the first Greek shipowner to own an oil tanker. During the building of the Ariston he meets the Norwegian whaling operator Anders Jahre and takes a small share in a whaling collier.

Chapter 6 — Tina Livanos, Niarchos and the surplus-ship indictment (pp. 65–83)

Opens on Eva Perón’s yacht trip in early 1947 with Alberto Dodero, in which Onassis refuses to buy the Argentine Navigation Line (nationalised a year later, wiping out Fritz Mandl’s investment). Brady then goes back to Onassis’s decision to marry a Greek woman and to Stavros Livanos’s daughters Tina and Eugenie, first met at the Plaza on 17 April 1943. Onassis marries Tina; Niarchos marries Eugenie six months later, and the brothers-in-law’s lifelong rivalry begins (Onassis forbids Tina to attend the Niarchos wedding). Livanos’s gifts include the Sutton Square townhouse.

After the war Onassis tries to buy U.S. Liberty ships using other people’s money. He gets National City Bank credit, uses a fixed-price long-term charter from Mobil to get a 2milliontankerfinancedbyMetropolitanLife,addsTexaco,ChaseManhattanandChemicalBank,andhasfivetankersunderconstruction(40 million) without advancing his own cash. Because the Ship Sales Act of 1946 reserves surplus ships for U.S. citizens, he and Niarchos set up American-controlled corporations (Onassis’s United States Petroleum Carriers, Inc., 49 percent his) with help from the “Casey group”. A Senate inquiry (1951–52), a grand jury and McCarthy’s allegations about cargoes to Communist China follow; the indictment is returned in 1953 and made public in February 1954. Chapter 8 — Monte Carlo and Prince Rainier (pp. 98–113)

Opens on a quotation from Prince Rainier about Onassis’s changed attitude. In spring 1951 Onassis and the 21-year-old Tina rent the Château de la Croë (“Chateau de la Croe” in the text) at Cap d’Antibes, formerly the Duke of Windsor’s; children Alexander and Christina, parties for film stars, the friendship with Greta Garbo, and a marriage drifting apart (Tina’s attachment to Reinaldo Herrera, his own affairs, under 90 days a year together). He seeks office space in Monaco for Olympic Maritime, is refused by the Société des Bains de Mer, and instead buys control through Paris Bourse purchases, with the consent of Rainier (a 20 percent holder), in January 1953. The New York Times headline of 16 January 1953 makes him famous. He hires public-relations men (Nigel Nielson, John W. Meyer), and in late 1954 joins Laurance Rockefeller and William Zeckendorf in an abortive $400 million offer for Howard Hughes’s holdings.

Brady covers Rainier’s near-bankruptcy in July 1955, his marriage to Grace Kelly (April 1956), the wedding-week parties on the Christina, Monaco’s tax-haven boom and De Gaulle’s objections, and the divergence between Onassis’s jet-set plans and Grace’s cultural ones. Disputes follow: the Niarchos share-purchase quarrel (arbitrated, Onassis paying 132,428),Rainier’sconstitutionalvetooverS.B.M.appointments,his1965attackin∗LeMonde∗,andthe1967MonegasquelawforcingS.B.M.toissue600,000sharesandbuyoutOnassisat16 a share. He receives $10 million on 17 March 1967 (“We were gypped”) and the friendship with the Rainiers ends.

Chapter 9 — Whaling and the war with Peru (pp. 114–124)

Opens with a party of Onassis’s guests watching a blue whale butchered aboard the factory ship Olympic Challenger in the Antarctic. Onassis forms the Olympic Whaling Company in partnership with the First German Whaling Company, registers it in Panama, converts Canadian frigates, and sails on 20 October 1950. Brady says the fleet broke International Whaling Commission rules from the first expedition: hunting before and after the season, outside permitted areas, taking cows, calves and undersize whales, using helicopters as spotters, and hiding behind icebergs under radio silence; the German crew protested. The first year nets $8.5 million. At the I.W.C. Tokyo meeting of June 1954 he is called an “international outlaw”; he blames Norwegian hostility, and refuses a British offer of free whale oil in exchange for withdrawing.

In August–November 1954 the fleet hunts sperm whales off Peru. On 15 November Peruvian destroyers seize several catchers within the claimed 200-mile zone and Peruvian aircraft bomb the Challenger, which surrenders; the ship’s log shows nearly 3,000 whales taken. Peru fines the company 3million,paidon13December;a14 million Lloyd’s policy with war-risk and detention cover (and 30,000adaywhiledetained)meansOnassiswas,inBrady’saccount,notfinanciallyoutofpocket.BritainandGermanyprotest;theU.S.eventuallyacceptsPeru’s200−mileclaim.ThePeruvianshipsthatseizedhishadbeensoldtoPerubyOnassishimself.HesellsthewhalingfleettoJapanfor8.5 million at the end of the 1955 season.

Chapter 10 — The Christina, Olympic Airways and Winston Churchill (pp. 125–143)

Opens on Onassis working at night in his cabin on the Christina, with his secretary Jenny Rocca-Serra below. Brady narrates Karamanlis’s 1957 invitation to take over Greece’s national airline and build a shipyard; Niarchos lobbies Queen Frederica and the compromise gives Onassis the airline (renamed Olympic Airways, about 15millioninitialinvestment,profitby1963)andNiarchostheSkaramangashipyard.In1965–66thePapandreougovernmentrefusesa30 million loan, Boeing finances three 707s, and transatlantic service opens in 1966; Brady reports the confrontation in which Papandreou threw him out after he asked for guaranteed returns and offered $1 million to the Center Union.

The longest part is a room-by-room tour of the Christina, rebuilt from the Canadian frigate Stormont at Kiel (325 ft, launched June 1954, over $4 million): the mosaic pool floor, the Game Room with Smyrna rugs and the whale-skin bar stools, the nine island-named guest suites, the Vertès murals story, the El Greco, the two-way mirror to the bathroom, 42 telephones, a seaplane, a crew of 65 and a steady stream of famous guests. Then the friendship with Winston Churchill, begun at a 1957 lunch in Monte Carlo arranged through Randolph Churchill: caviar fed by spoon, bezique and baccarat, a baklava flown in from Athens, the Caribbean cruise of 1960, a watercolor Churchill painted for him. Brady reads the bond partly as father-and-son compensation, and notes Onassis’s private criticism of Yalta and decolonisation.

Chapter 11 — Maria Callas and the end of the Livanos marriage (pp. 144–157)

Opens at the Covent Garden Medea party for Callas in London, then gives her background (Brooklyn birth in 1923, Meneghini, La Scala). In summer 1959, on a cruise on the Christina with Tina, the Churchills, the Meneghinis, Gianni and Marella Agnelli and others, Onassis and Callas fall in love; Meneghini rants at Istanbul and the cruise is cut short. Press conferences follow, with Meneghini’s “I created Callas” and Callas’s denials. Onassis is seen in Athens with Melina Mercouri (the plate-smashing night at the Kanaki Tavern). Tina leaves the yacht, sues for divorce in New York (naming only a “Mrs. J. R.”), reconciliation efforts by Grace, Lady Churchill and Livanos fail, and she eventually obtains the divorce in Alabama on grounds of mental cruelty. She marries the Marquess of Blandford, then, after Eugenie’s death from an overdose, Niarchos; Onassis snubs her at Churchill’s funeral.

The chapter ends with the 1967 London trial in which Callas and Onassis sued the shipowner Vergottis (“Pahaghis” in the OCR text and index) over her 25 shares in the tanker Artemesion II; Vergottis airs the pair’s private life, and the court finds for Onassis and Callas on two hearings.

Chapter 12 — Jacqueline Kennedy: courtship and marriage (pp. 158–183)

Opens with Jackie reading Robert Payne on the light of Greece on Skorpios two days after the wedding. Brady then traces the acquaintance: a 1950s Georgetown dinner, a visit with Senator Kennedy to the Christina at Monte Carlo to meet Churchill, her first Greek trip as First Lady with the Radziwills, Onassis’s interest in Lee Radziwill, and the 1963 Christina cruise after the death of her infant son Patrick (Franklin D. Roosevelt Jr. as escort; Congressman Oliver Bolton’s criticism; John Kennedy’s phone calls; Onassis’s remark that Jackie “is the captain”). After the assassination he is a White House house guest at the funeral; Robert Kennedy has him sign a document pledging half his wealth to Latin American poor.

For 1963–68 they meet privately. Onassis proposes during a spring 1968 Caribbean cruise; Jackie delays at Robert Kennedy’s request, then Robert is assassinated on 5 June 1968 and, in the biographer Frischauer’s reading that Brady quotes, she resolves to leave. Ted Kennedy flies to Skorpios, and a 173-clause marriage contract is negotiated through Andre Meyer; Brady lists terms from Christian Cafarakis’s recollection (a 3millionbondpayment,10 million per year of marriage, 20millionifsheleaveswithinfiveyears,100 million at his death, separate bedrooms). Her mother announces the engagement on 17 October 1968, and the wedding follows within days at the chapel of Panayitsa on Skorpios, with press kept 1,100 yards away. Worldwide reaction (Callas: “First I lost my weight, then I lost my voice, now I lost Onassis”), the Catholic question and Cardinal Cushing’s defence close the chapter.

Chapter 13 — The marriage, the junta and the novels (pp. 184–197)

Opens on a dinner party at Jackie’s Fifth Avenue apartment with Frank Sinatra and Mayor Lindsay. The children are openly cool: Alexander says “My father needed a wife but I certainly didn’t need a stepmother” and Christina calls Jackie “my father’s unhappy compulsion.” Days after the wedding Onassis leaves for Athens to negotiate a 400million(possibly600–700 million) investment package with the junta under Papadopoulos: an oil refinery, an aluminium smelter, a thermoelectric plant, a shipyard and resorts. The colonels play him against Niarchos, who offers a $100 million twenty-year loan and alleges irregularities; the tentative contract is cancelled within 24 hours and, after a secret Nixon meeting that Niarchos attacks as “exploiting,” the project never proceeds.

The marriage’s early phase includes dinners with Rose Kennedy, an arrangement in which Jackie keeps New York escorts, John and Caroline’s summers on Skorpios, Jackie’s fortieth birthday on 28 July 1969, and a night at the Neraida club. Brady then surveys the novels based on Onassis (The Greek, The Other Side of Midnight, Dolores, The Bourlotas Fortune) and the 1977 Greek Tycoon film. The chapter ends with Christina’s 1971 marriage to Joseph Bolker (objected to partly because he is Jewish and Onassis feared his oil connections), his failed attempt to buy out Harland and Wolff (he held 26 percent and was bought out for $250,000), tax-driven arrangements about the Pierre suite and a fourth-anniversary party at El Morocco in autumn 1972.

Chapter 14 — Alexander’s death, decline and Onassis’s death (pp. 198–212)

Opens with Onassis’s statement after his son’s death. In 1973 the oil boom lifts tanker rates from about 2.5millionto8 million a voyage and he makes over 100millionintheyear,thenordersabout360 million of new tankers (including two 426,000-ton super-supertankers); the October 1973 Arab oil embargo leaves them unneeded, he cancels at a 12millionlossplus5 million on another, and his assets fall by about half (roughly 1billionto500 million) by end of 1974. Alexander, Director of Island Services at Olympic Airways, crashes in a twin-engine light aircraft (“Piazzo” in the text) at Athens on 27 January 1973 (the book gives the pilot as Donald MacCusker). Onassis orders life support withdrawn, offers a $500,000 reward for proof of sabotage and, after a trial of the pilots, nothing is proved.

The remaining pages cover Katina Paxinou’s death, the Egyptian “safari,” the surrender of Olympic Airways to the government, quarrels with Jackie (the Acapulco trip, the lost 300,000marketinvestment,theGalellalawsuitcostingover200,000), his eighteen-page will and Greek law of 24 June 1974 voiding foreign-signed marriage contracts, and consultations with the lawyer Roy Cohn about divorce. Myasthenia gravis, Tina’s death in October 1974, influenza in January 1975, hospitalisation at the American Hospital in Paris, a gallbladder operation, Christina’s engagement to Peter Goulandris announced at his bedside, and his death on Saturday 15 March 1975 with only Christina present. Jackie’s statement thanks him for having “rescued” her.

Afterword (pp. 213–214)

A list of contradictions: gentle with children but violent with those he loved; generous to friends but a stingy tipper; no government allegiance but dealings with every Greek regime; hostile to and dependent on the press; supported fifty relatives and employed over ten thousand Greeks but rarely gave to charity; insured everything but claimed he could start over; wanted “what he could not purchase—the mercy of the gods.”

Index (pp. 215–218)

Names, ships and companies with page references (e.g. Callas, Churchill, Christina, ARAMCO, Niarchos, Dedichen, Kennedy family, S.B.M.). Useful for locating minor figures such as Maheu, Catapodis, Kulukundis, Casey and Alireza.

Notes for use

  • Authorial voice. Brady writes as a biographer for a general readership and reconstructs thoughts and conversations. Statements about Onassis’s interior states and many quoted dialogues should be treated as the author’s narration based on interviews and earlier books, not as documents.
  • Named secondary sources. Frischauer (Onassis), Fred Sparks, Joachim Joesten, Doris Lilly (Those Fabulous Greeks), Peter Hawkins (on Rainier), Nancy Holmes (The Dream Boats), Lord Moran, William Manchester, Benjamin Bradlee, Rose Kennedy’s Times to Remember, Dorothy Schiff, and Peter Evans are quoted for specific episodes.
  • Hedges the author flags. Examples: the claim that Socrates suspected Aristotle of diverting ransom money is labelled “pure speculation”; the Greek royal family’s stake in the Skaramanga shipyard is given as “my belief, based on interviews”; the source of Peru’s $3 million fine is reported as conflicting; the Maheu/C.I.A. bribery story ends with no proof; the Aristotle–Eva Perón affair is “a heavily circulated rumor.”
  • Figures. Fortune estimates (30 million in 1945, over 100 million in 1953, about 1 billion in 1973, about 500 million end-1974) and Suez profits are given as estimates.
  • Dating. Brady’s dates for the Tina marriage are loose: they meet on 17 April 1943 (she is fourteen), the Eva Perón yacht scene in “the first weeks of 1947” has the marriage not yet a month old, and by the divorce Tina speaks of “almost thirteen years.” Several chapters give years but not months (the Jackie cruise, the Kennedy visits, the Harland and Wolff bid).
