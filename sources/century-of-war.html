A Century of War: Anglo-American Oil Politics and the New World Order (Engdahl)

A long geopolitical history arguing that control of world petroleum has been the decisive stake of Anglo-American grand strategy from the late nineteenth-century British Empire through two world wars, the interwar oil settlements, Bretton Woods, the 1970s oil shocks, and early twenty-first-century wars in the Middle East and Central Asia.

Summary

F. William Engdahl’s A Century of War presents twentieth- and early twenty-first-century international politics as a continuous struggle to secure and control world oil reserves. The narrative begins with Britain’s late-Victorian crisis of free-trade empire—sterling, sea power, and raw-materials monopoly as the three “pillars”—and follows British, then Anglo-American, oil and financial policy through the First World War, the Middle Eastern settlement, the Federal Reserve and New York’s challenge to the City of London, interwar German and Soviet oil contests, the rise of Hitler as (in Engdahl’s account) a British geopolitical instrument, postwar decolonisation and Mossadegh, Gaullist Europe, Eurodollars, the 1973 oil shock and petrodollar order, IMF debt crises, and post–Cold War energy geopolitics culminating in the “war on terror” and contest with Russian energy power.

The local extract is a revised English edition published by edition.engdahl (Wiesbaden), with a 2004 preface framing the Iraq war as oil geopolitics and a 2011 apparatus including appendices on the Trilateral Commission’s 1973 founding members and a May 1973 Bilderberg meeting at Saltsjöbaden. The book is organized in thirteen chapters plus two appendices. Chapter titles and section headings below follow the table of contents and body openers in the extract; the work is a continuous argumentative history rather than an archival monograph, and summaries are drawn from body text, not the TOC alone.

Chapters

Preface (2004)

Engdahl frames the post-9/11 wars and the 2003 Iraq invasion as the latest phase of a century-long contest over oil. He presents the book as an account of how Anglo-American policy elites used oil control, finance, and geopolitics—drawing on Halford Mackinder’s world-island logic—to shape the international order, and he positions the revised edition as an update that carries that thesis into the early twenty-first century.

Chapter 1 — The Three Pillars of the British Empire

“No other element has shaped the history of the past one hundred years so much as the fight to secure and control the world’s reserves of petroleum.” Britain at the end of the 1890s is described as pre-eminent in politics, arms, and economy, yet rotting internally. Engdahl names three pillars of British power after Waterloo: naval and shipping supremacy; City of London credit and bills of exchange under the Bank of England and sterling; and geopolitical domination of major raw materials. Free trade after the Corn Law repeal (1846), The Economist, and a cheap-labour imperial system are presented as the economic strategy that enriched merchant banks (including opium-trade profits) while undermining domestic industry and agriculture. “Informal empire,” client states, balance of power, and the fusion of City commercial power with secret intelligence complete the late-Victorian picture that, in Engdahl’s account, sets up the turn to oil.

Chapter 2 — The Great Depression of 1873 and the End of Easy Leadership

The free-trade transformation and Bank of England interest-rate policy after the 1857 panic are blamed for industrial underinvestment and the Great Depression that hit Britain from the early 1870s. Foreign lending collapses; British manufactures lose ground at international exhibitions; by the 1890s easy industrial leadership is over. Malthusian free-trade dogma is declared doomed, and the elite debate over how to maintain empire opens the door to geopolitical remedies—setting the stage for the oil century.

Chapter 3 — A Global Fight for Control of Petroleum Begins

Germany’s industrial, shipping, and naval rise after unification is contrasted with Britain’s retardation. German merchant tonnage, Hamburg–American and North German Lloyd concentration, Tirpitz’s navy laws, and the Dreadnought race make Germany, in British eyes, the principal threat. Sir Llewellyn Woodward is quoted on Germany’s right to a navy commensurate with its trade. By about 1910, Engdahl argues, dramatic remedies—including war—were under discussion in London as answers to German economic emergence.

Chapter 4 — Oil Becomes the Weapon, the Great War

The chapter develops pre-1914 British strategy against Germany: fear of Continental economic strength, the Fashoda crisis, the disruption of Hanotaux’s Franco-German normalisation, the Dreyfus Affair’s political use, and the Entente Cordiale under Delcassé. Oil enters as the strategic resource of modern fleets and armies. British policy is presented as steering Europe toward a war that would break German industrial power and secure Middle Eastern oil for Britain and its allies.

Chapter 5 — Combined and Uneven: Versailles and the Redrawing of the Map

Post-1918 Middle Eastern diplomacy: Sykes–Picot, contradictory promises to the Arabs (Husain, Lawrence), “selling the same horse twice,” zones of temporary military occupation, and Lloyd George’s private claim on Mosul oil. Arthur Balfour’s letter to Lord Rothschild is treated as part of the same settlement logic. Britain’s roughly one-million-strong wartime Middle Eastern military presence underwrites the carve-up that places key oil zones under British or British-aligned control despite wartime pledges.

Chapter 6 — The Anglo-Americans Close Ranks

U.S. entry into the war, the Federal Reserve Act (1913), Liberty Loans, and the rise of Morgan and New York finance are presented as shifting the centre of world credit. Thomas Lamont, Bernard Baruch, and John Foster Dulles at Versailles; war-debt politics; and the founding of coordinated Anglo-American institutes—Lionel Curtis, the Royal Institute of International Affairs, and the New York Council on Foreign Relations—frame the 1920s as a decade of bitter yet interlocking struggle over debts, rubber, naval parity, and sterling–dollar relations, ending in a working Anglo-American condominium over oil and finance.

Chapter 7 — Oil and the New World Order of the 1920s

Rapallo (1922) shocks Genoa: German–Soviet economic rapprochement threatens British hopes of exclusive access to Russian oil. Shell’s Deterding, Churchill’s support for White counter-revolution, the Lockhart–Reilly intrigues, and the later shift to trade opening are set against American bids via Harry Sinclair and Standard interests. The chapter maps interwar contests for Caucasian and Middle Eastern oil within the new Anglo-American order.

Chapter 8 — Banking, Depression, and the ‘Hitler Project’

The 1931 financial crisis, Creditanstalt, Montagu Norman, and the credit squeeze on Germany lead into Engdahl’s account of Hjalmar Schacht’s resignation from the Reichsbank and alleged organisation of finance for Hitler. British policy is said to have fostered a “Hitler Project” as a geopolitical instrument against the Soviet Union and the European balance; American consular and military reporting on early National Socialism is cited to show elite awareness of the movement’s character.

Chapter 9 — A New Empire and a New World War

Wartime and immediate postwar oil politics, the preservation of British stakes in Persia/Iran, and the transition into the Cold War oil order. The chapter bridges Second World War outcomes to the 1950s crises of formal empire and the defence of Anglo-Iranian oil.

Chapter 10 — Europe, Japan and a Response to the Oil Shock / Postwar Continuities

(Body sequence in the extract moves through Mossadegh and Anglo-Iranian Oil, European recovery and the Common Market, de Gaulle–Adenauer independence, Kennedy-era Anglo-American design against European autonomy, dollar drain, Eurodollars, and sterling’s weakness.) Mossadegh’s challenge to Anglo-Iranian monopoly, BBC propaganda, and refusal of 50–50 terms set up the 1953 overthrow as oil geopolitics. De Gaulle’s return, force de frappe, and Franco-German accords alarm Washington and London. Eurodollar markets and City revival after Kennedy-era capital controls are presented as restoring London’s financial role inside a dollar system under strain—preface to the 1970s oil shock.

Chapter 11 — Imposing the New World Order

Paul Volcker’s interest-rate shock as a “British model”; gunboat diplomacy and Mexico; IMF conditionality as a 1920s replay; Reagan-era consequences; the fall of the Berlin Wall as a panic for some circles; Saddam Hussein, Desert Storm, and the “New World Order”; and the targeting of an independent Europe and Japan. Petrodollar recycling and debt crisis discipline the developing world and reassert Anglo-American financial power after the 1973–74 price explosion (treated in the preceding oil-shock material: Bilderberg 1973, Kissinger and the Yom Kippur war, OPEC’s 70 percent rise).

Chapter 12 — From Evil Empire to the Axis of Evil

After the Soviet collapse, a search for a new bogeyman; the wounding of Japan as lead “flying goose”; the Asian financial crisis as “hunting Asian Tigers”; Washington’s return to Mackinder; Russia under IMF “third world cure”; Yugoslavia and Balkan oil geopolitics. The chapter argues that post–Cold War interventions continue the same energy and financial logic under new ideological labels.

Chapter 13 — A New Millennium for Oil Geopolitics

Bush and the return of big oil to Washington; “where the prize ultimately lies”; the Project for the New American Century; war on terror versus war on oil from Kabul to Baghdad; Putin’s energy card and efforts to control Russian spigots; bases and “full spectrum dominance.” The Iraq and Afghan wars are read as contests over pipelines, reserves, and strategic denial rather than solely counter-terrorism.

Appendix I — Founding Members of the Trilateral Commission (1973)

List of founding members, offered as documentation of the elite network Engdahl associates with 1970s oil and monetary redesign.

Appendix II — Saltsjöbaden Bilderberg Meeting, May 11–13, 1973

Partial list of participants (including oil and banking figures such as Robert O. Anderson and Lord Greenhill of BP), used to support the claim that a small Anglo-American circle planned a major assault on industrial growth via oil-price strategy before the October 1973 war.
