The City: London and the Global Power of Finance (Norfield)

A City-insider political economy of British imperialism after 1945, treating finance as the nervous system of capitalism and London as the world’s broker: eurodollars, FX and OTC derivatives, current-account dealing revenues, tax-haven spokes, and an Anglo-American “double planet” that appropriates value from a ranked hierarchy of states.

Summary

Tony Norfield’s The City (Verso, 2016) is not a dealing-room memoir. It uses a 1987 Bank of America eurobond-desk anecdote and nearly twenty years in London banks (American, Japanese, British, Dutch) to ask how the global financial system works and in whose interests. Finance is not a cancer removable from a healthy “productive” capitalism; it is inseparable from production, mergers, and state debt. Britain uses the system to appropriate value while appearing to provide a service. Three themes run through the book: a small group of states holds privileged positions in production, commerce, investment, and finance; finance pervades all economic activity rather than sitting beside a “real economy”; and UK external accounts and investment flows show how the City finances a chronic goods deficit and funds British capital worldwide. Norfield rejects treating the City as Wall Street’s satellite (Gowan, Panitch/Gindin), as a City–Bank–Treasury clique against industry, or as “financialisation” plus the 2007–8 crash. The US is hegemonic but not first in every market; the UK is first in international banking location, FX turnover, and OTC interest-rate derivatives. A five-measure “pecking order” for 2013–14 ranks the US far ahead, the UK second (index 34 versus US 96), then China. Later chapters track leverage and profit, seigniorage and dollar-payments power, the UK current and financial accounts, and China’s challenge via the NDB, AIIB, and offshore renminbi.

Chapters

Preface

Summer 1987: the head of eurobond trading at Bank of America International in London asks the new analyst “Where is value?” Less than a year after Thatcher’s Big Bang, foreign banks are expanding in the City. Norfield’s career lasts nearly twenty years, three further banks (Japanese, British, Dutch), dealing rooms, and travel to forty countries. Screens improve; the search for value does not. If the dealer finds it, a portion becomes bonus and bank profit. The casino analogy is misleading: the City’s primary role is managing deals for capitalist companies and governments—the financial nerve centre of the global system, first in line when crises hit.

The book is not a City autobiography. The City does not exist only to launch a business elite, though it can do that. It plays particular roles for British capitalism and could not do so unless it also serviced a global system. It generates dealing revenues from worldwide transactions and easy funding for favoured corporations and governments, facilitating finance and centralising economic power.

A second aim is to refuse the counter-position of finance to a favoured “productive” capitalism. Financial operations arise from market production; large “productive” firms merge, take over rivals, and use equity and bond markets. If you dislike finance but accept the capitalist market, the two are inseparable.

Chapter 1 — Britain, Finance and the World Economy

Admiral Lord West (September 2011) rejects “second-tier power” status: fifth or sixth wealthiest nation, large export share of GDP, world shipping run from the UK, largest European investor in South and Southeast Asia and the Pacific Rim, permanent UN Security Council member—“not bloody Denmark or Belgium.” Norfield notes five wars under Labour 1997–2010 and Libya, but focuses on economic foundations West omitted: corporate ownership and financial markets.

2013 outward FDI stock: UK 1,885bn,secondtoUS6,350bn, larger as a share of the national economy. FT Top 500 (2011): UK second, 34 companies, 2,085bnmarketvalueversusUS160companies,9,602bn. Of the world’s top 100 non-financial corporations (2013, foreign assets): 23 US, 16 British, 11 French, 10 each Germany and Japan; the three biggest UK-based were Royal Dutch/Shell, BP, Vodafone (2nd, 6th, 7th). Top 50 financial companies by geographical spread (2012): UK second with six institutions to US ten; HSBC in 65 countries, Barclays 46. Canada, France, Germany, Switzerland each had four. UK-based bank assets more than four times GDP; adding equities and debt securities, eight times GDP. Only smaller niches (Switzerland, Luxembourg, Ireland) are larger relative to the domestic economy.

Finance is not confined to banks. Ford Motor Credit and GE Capital illustrate industrial firms’ finance arms; takeovers, equity stakes, and buyout ambitions are typical. States sell securities and central banks set rates. Under imperialism—defined as the stage where a few corporations from a few countries dominate the world market—access to finance both reflects and retains power. Some 200 countries exist; about twenty count; among those a hierarchy. Birth in a rich country is the global analogue of wealthy parents.

The financial system cannot be understood nationally. The dollar is US national money and “world money”; Fed decisions hit the globe, and world flows condition US markets.

Britain’s “invisible empire” after 1979 and especially Big Bang 1986 has lacked a substantial radical review since the 1980s for three reasons: finance is treated as outside power; US dominance (Britain as “lapdog” over Iraq) crowds out British imperial interest; City prominence is treated as policy choice rather than outgrowth of imperial power. Cain and Hopkins’s British Imperialism barely covers post-1970.

International investment income dwarfs many national GDPs: 2013 US receipts 773.4bn,payments564.9bn, net about $209bn. UK revenues come more from international dealing than from foreign investments. Norfield criticises Peter Gowan (The Global Gamble) for a US-only view and for treating crises as planned; the City is not merely “a servicing centre for the dollar currency zone and a satellite of Wall Street.” Hedge funds bet on 1997 Asia from assessments of deficits and short-term capital, not US government design. Eric Helleiner is stronger on multiple states and industrial lobbying for capital-account opening, but treats policy as intellectual influence and Britain’s liberalisation as a “lagging” hegemonic commitment to nineteenth-century finance. Norfield: late-twentieth-century UK policy was a forward-looking leverage of the City’s existing status.

Panitch and Gindin (The Making of Global Capitalism) overstate consensus and US Treasury/Fed/IMF political power, understate economic appropriation (dollar funding of deficits as subsidy), and recycle the satellite thesis. US dollar LIBOR is not “the internationally traded version of the Fed’s interest rate”: the US has no equivalent unsecured term interbank market—no “NYIBOR.” They treat latest US hegemony as durable (“End of History”). Norfield notes euro origins in 1970s dollar chaos, possible future euro-area defensive measures, UK EU-referendum politics (2016–17), UKIP 13 per cent in 2015, and China: 2014 New Development Bank (Shanghai, China-funded) and AIIB (US failed to keep allies out); China/Hong Kong/Shanghai/Shenzhen already second in equity capitalisation and turnover.

David Harvey’s “accumulation by dispossession” and crisis focus end in a call for a new “New Deal” and “more benevolent imperial trajectory,” sidelining daily financial appropriation. François Chesnais’s stress on concentrated interest- and dividend-bearing money-capital as legally protected claims on surplus is accepted; high borrowing costs for weak countries are only a small part of broader appropriation.

Roadmap: chs. 2–3 historical US–UK evolution and Europe; ch. 4 institutions, credit, securities prices; ch. 5 hierarchy; ch. 6 profit and leverage; ch. 7 privileges by country; ch. 8 UK machine; ch. 9 Islamic finance, China, and a changing environment for Anglo-American domination.

Chapter 2 — The Anglo-American System

1925 gold-standard return at the 1914 sterling parity is used to show that financial policy cannot be read from the national economy alone. Keynes’s Economic Consequences of Mr Churchill treated only domestic overvaluation (~10 per cent versus the dollar) and ignored the government’s US/Empire rationale. Churchill: if Britain did not move, the Empire would go onto a gold standard of the dollar. Budget speech: US and Germany already gold-related; Dominions in “complete unity”; a “uniform standard of value” like ships in a harbour. The experiment lasted until 1931 gold outflows; then protectionist Empire/Dominion links and, from 1940 to the early 1970s, the Sterling Area.

Bretton Woods (July 1944): US industrial and reserve supremacy. Morgenthau wanted the financial centre in New York, not London, and placed IMF and World Bank in Washington. New York did not achieve the domination envisaged: US banks faced Glass-Steagall, interstate limits, Regulation Q (no interest on demand deposits; rate caps until the 1980s), and the 1963 Interest Equalisation Tax on US residents’ purchases of foreign securities. London specialised in short-term credit, foreign bonds, and a developed unsecured term interbank market. Time zone: London covers Asia morning, Europe all day, Americas afternoon; New York misses Tokyo/Singapore days and the European morning. Even in 1957 sterling still financed some 40 per cent of world trade.

The “special relationship” was not mutual support. US pressure ended the Anglo-Japanese alliance in the 1920s; Lend-Lease and asset liquidation bought US funds and bases; Keynes’s bancor was rejected. Suez 1956: US opposition plus sterling selling forced Britain down. Post-1945 Britain still tried to restore Empire (including using Japanese troops in Asia; Indian troops in colonial restoration). Cold War softened US anti-colonialism; Cain and Hopkins: the Empire became a “bulwark against the Communist menace.” Convertibility attempt 1947 failed; 1949 devaluation 4.03to2.80; many FX restrictions until 1958.

Sterling Area: colonies deposited trade earnings in London at a fixed rate and could not freely draw dollars even when they earned them; Dominions had more leeway. Marketing boards bought colonial commodities below world prices (Acland 1951: West African palm oil £94/ton versus free market £134–210). Harold Wilson 1953: dollar problems might be solved “200 or 1,000 feet below the soil in the Colonial areas.” Jennie Lee 1956: keep them as bankers. Groundnut scheme abandoned 1951. By the late 1950s Western European recovery made Empire orientation passé. Colonies began running US deficits; Sterling Area current-account deficit with the world rose from average £508m (1958–61) to £754m (1962–65), a little over 2 per cent of UK GDP, while the UK itself had a modest surplus. Empire deficits added to UK financing trouble. EFTA 1960; first EEC application 1961, de Gaulle veto January 1963 after Nassau Polaris deal with Kennedy.

Pre-1914 Britain had current-account surplus averaging 5 per cent of GDP; ~1910 one-third of world securities quoted in London, foreign securities ~60 per cent of London listings. War sales and exchange controls until 1958 destroyed that. Radcliffe Report 1959: City “invisible earnings” need not collapse if settlements moved off sterling.

Euromarkets: eurocurrency deposits/loans from late 1950s; eurobonds from 1963 after Bank of England 1962 allowed foreign-currency securities in London. “Euro” means outside national regulation, usually with non-residents; transfers of dollars still clear through the US payments system. Interest paid gross; no FDIC on eurodollar deposits in US banks. Fed imposed 10 per cent reserve on extra eurodollar borrowings October 1969; market still grew. Deposits estimated from ~1bn(1960)to57bn (1970) to 1,050bn(1983); 90percentdollarsinthe1960s.BankofEnglandGovernor,Chicago1971:eurodollarmarketequaltoFrance’smoneysupply;roll−overcredits,FRNs,infanteurodollarcommercialpaperforcorporationswhoseneedsdomesticsystemscouldnotmeet.USFDI 3bn/year early 1960s, $6bn by decade’s end.

UK net foreign invisible earnings from financial services more than quadrupled 1958–70 to £439m (0.4 to 0.8 per cent of GDP), offsetting the visible deficit and cushioning stop-go. Bank of England welcomed the market; US banks in London from 9 (1963) to 31 (c. 1971); 160 banks from 48 countries by 1971. 1963: American banks and British overseas/Commonwealth banks each ~25 per cent of London market business; accepting houses under 20 per cent. US banks nearly half of foreign-bank liabilities late 1960s. ~30 per cent of euro deposits and 40 per cent of loans in the 1970s longer than three months—hence LIBOR, no NYIBOR.

Eurobonds: bearer, no tax deduction; IET pushed issuance from New York. By 1968, 60 per cent of eurocurrency-bond trading in London ($15m/day). Early 2000s: 70 per cent of international bond trading in London; 2010 UK 13 per cent of international bonds outstanding (US 24 per cent) but 70 per cent of trading.

1975: UK 27 per cent of international banking, twice the US; Japan seventh, under 5 per cent. By 1989 Japan 17 per cent, Japanese banks over 35 per cent of London international banking by 1987. Plaza-era yen rise, BoJ ease, property boom, then crash: Japan’s international banking share below 10 per cent from 2000. London remained the dealing location even for Frankfurt and Amsterdam banks (ABN AMRO kept FX in Amsterdam, securities in London).

Historical logic: euromarkets served industry and commerce’s scale, encouraged by UK authorities, not impeded in substance by the US. Capital controls later relaxed in the 1980s because all kinds of capitalists demanded the right to invest for the best return. Those flows, plus shifting competitive power (US/UK relatively weaker, Germany/Japan stronger), undermined Bretton Woods. Britain shifted from Empire exploitation and sterling to dollar intermediation.

Chapter 3 — Finance and the Major Powers

1970s: end of postwar boom, slump in profitability, unemployment, inflation, fiscal deficits. UK: 500,000 unemployed a disgrace in the 1960s; below a million a “result” by late 1970s. Bretton Woods ends; international financial trading accelerates. UK and US use the financial system to manage relative decline. Britain’s non-European interests and US security/military alliance make it reluctant to become an also-ran in a German-dominated Europe.

Metaphor: not satellite but “double planet”—mutual gravitational pull, US larger. March 2015 US rebuke of UK joining AIIB. British state authority over City property rules, not government creation of markets, underpinned London’s postwar role. Burn is right that private firms pioneered euromarkets, wrong that a pre-industrial City clique was “restored.” Industry and finance are partners in exploitation.

Chapter 4 — Power and Parasitism

Holcim–Lafarge (2015): €41bn cement merger repriced after Swiss-franc jump—ten Lafarge shares for nine Holcim instead of 1:1. Finance rapidly reshapes corporate power. Commercial capital (Wal-Mart, Tesco) and money-dealing capital (Visa, FX, commercial paper) take cuts from value produced elsewhere; they persist only if more efficient than in-house dealing. Apple/Dell brand and outsource production.

Interest-bearing capital: M–M′, money advanced for surplus and returned with interest—loans, shares, bonds—outside the commodity circuit. Money-capitalists include rich individuals, boards, fund managers, oligarchs, not only banks. Banks: payments (HSBC 1.9bnUSfine2012),deposits/loans,money−marketandFXdealing,proprietaryrisk,longer−termloansandprimarysecurities(interest−bearing),overdrafts(money−dealing).Brokerstakefees/spreads.Assetmanagers(BlackRock4.6tn, 30 countries, clients in 100) take AUM fees; hedge funds also take profit share. Insurers (Berkshire Hathaway) invest premiums; worker premiums become capital via insurers and pension funds (Aviva; Dutch ABP nearly $400bn). From early 2000s pension funds entered commodity futures.

Banks create money by lending: fictitious deposits, settled net in interbank systems (CHAPS >£250bn/day in 2011). Reckoning with value production is stretched, not broken; weak countries snap sooner. Loans are assets only if repaid. Northern Rock 2007: interbank market closed before retail queues.

Securities are fictitious capital (Marx): equity prices discount expected earnings at market rates, not invested plant. Government debt has no underlying capital asset. Example: 5perpetual,5percentrates→100; 2 per cent → 250;10percent→50. Post-2008 rate cuts lifted equities despite weak output. Owners get votes/boards, creditor rights (states impose policy on default), and liquidity (sale, collateral, payment). Markets enforce discipline: sell shares, bonds, currency. Issuance and share swaps scale monopolies—“a new financial aristocracy” of promoters and speculators. CDOs claimed residential mortgage payments; when payments failed, prices collapsed.

Facebook 2012 IPO: 16bnraised,banksunder200m fees, market cap over 100bn;Zuckerberg18percenteconomic,57percentvotes.2014WhatsApp:22bn, only 4bncash,reststock;WhatsApplost139m (2013) and $232m (H1 2014).

Hilferding’s Finance Capital (1910) is criticised: banks do not command industry as in his Berlin-six-banks thesis; UK industry self-financed from Empire profits; fictitious capital is industrial ownership; national rather than world-market standpoint. Zaibatsu banks did not necessarily run the group; Rockefeller finance grew from oil, not bank domination. Political implication—control banks, tame capitalism—fails because monopolists cannot control the global market.

Parasitism: Marx did not call commercial/money-dealing capital parasitic because they facilitate the productive circuit; interest-bearing capital’s M–M′ sits outside it. Broader strata in rich countries own securities, not only a financial aristocracy.

Chapter 5 — The World Hierarchy

“Who runs the world?” is the US—but hegemony changes (pre-1914; interwar British political power; Korea stalemate; Vietnam loss) and the system is anarchic rivalry, not orderly management.

Chapter 6 — Profit and Finance

Profit originates in productive surplus; commerce, money-dealing, finance, and tax-financed state spending are deductions or reallocations, including from abroad.

Return on equity and leverage: if profit rate 10 per cent and borrow at 5 per cent, £100 equity + £200 debt → RoE 20 per cent. US manufacturing leverage 2001–10 averaged under 1. Banks create assets far above equity; “normal” leverage ~20×. Hedge funds lever via banks/margin. 1990s–2000s: US net interest margins from ~4.0–4.5 per cent to below 3.5 per cent by 2006 → volume and trading to protect profit → leverage. Chart 6.1: some majors over 100 (US) and over 80 (Europe) before the crash—four or five times “normal.” A 2 per cent asset loss at 50× leverage wipes equity. Chart 6.2: UK major-bank leverage fell in the 1960s, modest rise 1970–2000 around ~20, then historical highs into 2007–8, then back toward average as assets shrank and capital rose.

Low bond yields (4–6 per cent early 2000s vs ~7 per cent pension targets) pushed funds into commodity derivatives, property, forests, hedge funds. “London Whale” (Bruno Iksil, JP Morgan London, 2012): >$6bn credit-derivative loss after doubling up in a small market.

Hilferding’s claim that banking earns the average rate of profit is rejected: no equalisation mechanism between control of money-capital and productive capital; banks create assets; ONS has no “capital employed” profit rate for finance comparable to industry. UK bank licences ration entry (Metro Bank 2010: first new High Street bank in 100 years; 18 months for a deposit licence).

Bank assets largely short-term lending to the rest of finance and secondary securities, not new plant. UK 2011 GFCF £215bn versus under £23bn new UK equity issues. Table 6.1 (UK MFI financial balance sheet) and derivative notional-versus-value discussion support treating derivatives cautiously in national accounts.

US corporate profit rate (Chart 6.3, 1948–2013) and Fed holdings of Treasuries and mortgage securities (Chart 6.4) are used to argue that recorded US domestic profits include worldwide commercial and financial privilege, and that QE props asset prices. A country’s place in the hierarchy changes how profit data should be read.

Chapter 7 — The Imperial Web

Taibbi’s Goldman “vampire squid” is too narrow: the web is the whole financial system, an outgrowth of production and a means for powerful firms and states to extract value.

Dollar invoicing: ~100 per cent of US exports, over 90 per cent of imports; euro two-thirds of extra-euro exports, half of imports; UK and Japan over half of trade in foreign currencies. International commodities priced in dollars shift FX risk onto others.

ECB: euro notes outside the euro area €36.4bn mid-2003 to €143bn end-2013 (~1.5 per cent of euro GDP). UK seigniorage after Sterling Area dissolution is treated as negligible versus GDP. IMF 2012: 43 countries dollar-anchored (Ecuador dollarised); 27 non-members use the euro (Kosovo, Montenegro, Bulgaria board; 14 CFA states).

“Exorbitant privilege”: cheap own-currency funding and excess return on net foreign assets. Dollar ~two-thirds of official reserves. Table 7.2 (2013, $bn): US NYSE+NASDAQ capitalisation 24,035 / turnover 23,285; China Shanghai+Shenzhen+HK 7,050 / 9,665; Japan 4,543 / 6,516; UK LSE 4,429 / 2,315; Euronext BE/FR/NL/PT 3,584 / 1,722; Canada 2,114 / 1,333; Germany 1,936 / 1,383. US four or five times Japan; UK roughly twice Germany. Listing on a major market makes equity usable as world money for control.

Division of labour: US nearest to “full spectrum dominance”; UK often larger for its economy; Switzerland, Luxembourg, and smaller havens take niches.

Chapter 8 — Inside the Machine

London is the largest hub for FX, OTC derivatives, international bank lending/borrowing, and international-bond trading, even though the Fed sets the key rate and NY/Chicago dominate exchange-traded securities. Three roles: (1) international revenues offset the chronic goods deficit; (2) finance any payments gap cheaply and fund British outward investment; (3) give UK-based firms access to world funds. That mechanism is contemporary British imperialism.

Employment: just over two million in finance and related jobs, 7 per cent of UK employment; a little over one million in banking and insurance. 2011–12 financial-services tax £63bn, 12 per cent of the total. Darling: 2000s spending assumed those revenues and stamp duty would continue.

Net figures measure privilege (inflows minus outflows) but understate: without the City, Britain would import financial services. Banking flows are the residual that fills current-account gaps.

Nineteenth-century pattern: goods deficit up to 6 per cent of GDP, current-account surplus from shipping, insurance, investment income. Last UK current-account surplus 1983. Goods deficit again 6 per cent of GDP in 2014. Financial plus insurance services covered more than half the goods deficit 2008–14.

UK has the second-largest financial-services surplus after the US; with insurance, the largest (US insurance in deficit). Banks earn more from spreads than fees; securities dealers the reverse. Net financial-services earnings ~£29bn (2006), £36bn (2007), average £38bn (2013–14).

Chapter 9 — Eternal Interests, Temporary Allies

Palmerston, 1 March 1848: no eternal allies, no perpetual enemies; eternal interests. After 1945, and especially from the 1980s, Britain became the world’s broker—transaction revenues, not new capital export.

Mark Carney: “we are open for business”; finance a tenth of GDP, one million jobs, large exports; 2050 bank assets could reach 9× GDP if UK share of global banking holds. Martin Wolf called it a “big bet on finance.” Successive UK governments promote finance regardless of stated industrial priorities. EU financial-transaction-tax talk: UK opposed; CJEU rejected UK challenge June 2014; if levied on the location of a party, London could become Germany’s tax collector. LIBOR administration moved to NYSE Euronext; the market and calculation stay in London. Non-dom legal haven; oligarch property, clubs, papers. Post-Crimea 2014 briefing: do not “close London’s financial centre to Russians.” Barclays 2008 capital from Abu Dhabi and Qatar, over £7bn.

Vulnerabilities: rising external debt and current-account deficit since 2012 (investment-income outflows, fines, import rebound). Scottish referendum 2014: 55–45 remain—oil, nuclear base, military personnel, image of territorial security. EU referendum 2016–17: large corporations do not want exit; UKIP ~one-eighth of 2015 votes; mid-Atlantic policy (Common Market yes, euro no).

Islamic finance: interest formally prohibited; structures as shared risk or rent. Cameron, October 2013, World Islamic Economic Forum in London: want London alongside Dubai. £200m sukuk June 2014, first outside the Muslim world, ten times subscribed. ~1.5tnSharia−compliantassets;139bn sukuk issued 2012; 22 Islamic banks in the UK (six fully compliant), more than other western countries; $34bn sukuk listed on LSE. Task Force created. Oil-price fall after 2014 a risk to the funding base. Links: Saudi flows and arms, Qatari property, Brunei Gurkhas and Shell.

China/BRICS: 2013 China accounted for more than 80 per cent of the US 400bncurrent−accountdeficitbutalsosentmorethan200bn portfolio investment. NDB launched Shanghai 21 July 2015, China ~40 per cent of money, Indian then Brazilian presidency; 100bncapitalplus100bn contingency reserves. May 2014 Russia–China energy deal reported as not settled in dollars. BRICS are not a consistent bloc (US uses India versus China). 2013 GDP: China just over 9tn,India 2tn, Brazil 2.2tn,Russia2.1tn, South Africa 0.4tn.ChinaFXreservesover3.5tn. RMB ~14 per cent of Chinese exports by 2014; swap lines with many central banks; SWIFT share end-2014 ~2 per cent (USD 45, EUR 28, GBP 8), fifth, near Japan. City already ~60 per cent of offshore RMB trading outside China and Hong Kong (US 15, France 10). BoE–PBoC swap June 2013 CNY 200bn—half what was mooted, same as Australia, below ECB’s 350bn. RQFII quota CNY 80bn; dim-sum issuance; Chinese banks eased into London; October 2014 UK CNY 3bn sovereign (first western). March 2015 White House reprimand for UK “constant accommodation” as first G7 AIIB member; Germany, France, Italy followed; Australia and South Korea later. Obama April 2015: “If we don’t write the rules, China will write the rules out in that region.”

Close: financial prices are today’s laws of supply and demand. Do not analyse financialisation nationally or split bad banks from good industry. Marx’s vampire sucking living labour must be extended: relations have taken a financial form across industrial and commercial corporations of the leading powers. The City is a “vampire’s blood bank” taking a sip from values that flow through it; securities are claims on future value—unborn rather than undead. “Only a stake in the heart of the capitalist system, not simply in some of its financial forms, will be enough.”
