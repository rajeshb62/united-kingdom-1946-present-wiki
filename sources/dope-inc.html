Dope, Inc.: Britain’s Opium War Against the United States (1978)

A U.S. Labor Party investigative monograph that presents the world narcotics trade as a single British-centered business and political-warfare operation against the United States, from the nineteenth-century Opium Wars through Hong Kong banking, Canadian Prohibition networks, North American organized crime, and the 1960s drug-rock counterculture.

Summary

Commissioned in September 1978 by U.S. Labor Party National Chairman Lyndon H. LaRouche, Jr., and written by Konstandinos Kalimtgis, David Goldman, and Jeffrey Steinberg under his direction, Dope, Inc. is a long polemic and investigative report rather than a conventional academic history. Its thesis, stated at the outset, is that illegal drug traffic into the United States is a calculated form of political warfare by the British monarchy and London-centered finance, comparable to—and continuous with—the Opium Wars against China. The authors claim a combined research effort of about one hundred people in the U.S., Mexican, and European Labor Parties, with assistance from unnamed law-enforcement sources, and they frame the book as an extension of LaRouche’s October 1978 statement “A National Strategy to Control Crime.”

The work is organized in five parts. Part I retells British opium policy from the East India Company through Palmerston’s networks and American Prohibition. Part II maps what the authors call the production and wholesale finance of “Dope, Incorporated”—Golden Triangle opium, Hong Kong and the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank, gold and diamonds, a “Peking connection,” the Royal Institute of International Affairs, Canada as “North America’s Hong Kong,” and interlocking British oligarchic families. Part III treats North American distribution and “organized crime”: the Bronfmans, the Kennedys, Permindex and Resorts International/Intertel, the Jacobs family’s Emprise/Sportsystems, and a Philadelphia amphetamine case study. Part IV attributes the 1960s counterculture and LSD cult to British psychological warfare (Huxley, Tavistock, RIIA). Part V attacks the U.S. “drug lobby” for marijuana and heroin decriminalization. An epilogue records the authors’ 1978 presentations to Federal Reserve and New York banking officials against the HongShang–Marine Midland acquisition, and a Paris meeting attacked by Betar militants. Chapters

Dedication (Lyndon H. LaRouche, Jr., New York City, October 18, 1978)

LaRouche dedicates the book to U.S. intelligence and law-enforcement officers who have died or been imprisoned in the “silent war” against British illegal drug traffic into the United States, with thanks to American, French, and Canadian officials who helped cross-check facts. He states that the only proper comparison for today’s traffic is the British monarchy’s nineteenth-century Opium Wars against China, and that the same HongShang and related banking interests, “aided by those leading elements of the Zionist Lobby which have controlled organized crime in the USA and the Caribbean since the early 1920s,” remain involved. He presents the traffic as calculated political warfare and balance-of-payments looting, cites Bertrand Russell and Aldous Huxley on drugs as political subversion, and closes: the fight against illegal drugs and “decriminalization” is “nothing less than a war against Britain.”

Acknowledgments

The authors state that Dope, Inc. was commissioned in September 1978 by LaRouche and produced under his direction as part of an international Labor Party campaign against organized crime and drug traffic, linked in their account to defense of the European Monetary System and LaRouche’s 1975 International Development Bank proposal. The book is credited to a roughly 100-person research team in New York, Mexico City, and Wiesbaden; named research contributors include Marilyn James, Richard Schulman, Alfred Ross, and others, with Linda Frommer as editor. Law-enforcement sources are thanked without names.

Introduction

The book opens: “America is at war.” The battleground is American streets and schools; the “most deadly weapon is dope.” The authors cite retail U.S. drug sales of about 50billionandaworldwidetakeofabout200 billion, described as a slush fund for terrorism and the murder of American Presidents. They reject the idea that drug abuse is merely self-inflicted and name the controller of supply, banks, syndicates, and political corruption as “the aristocratic and banking oligarchy of Great Britain”—the same adversary as in the American Revolution and the founders of the world opium trade. DEA-style user statistics for heroin, cocaine, and marijuana are recited (including a claim of over 48 million frequent users of those three drugs). The introduction sets the polemic frame for the five parts that follow.

Part I — History

Introduction (Part I)

The Part I introduction compares America in 1978 to China on the eve of the first Opium War: foreign smuggling, mass addiction, brazen organized crime, assassinations, and shattered law enforcement. It quotes a dope-trafficker writing that a drug-addicted nation cannot become a military threat. A parallel is drawn between the assassinations of Lincoln and Kennedy as narcotics-mob and foreign-conspiracy cases. Nineteenth-century British finance and firms—Matheson, Keswick, Swire, Dent, Baring, Rothschild; Jardine Matheson; Hongkong and Shanghai Bank; Chartered Bank; P&O—and intelligence fronts (Order of Zion, Mazzini’s Mafia, Chinese Triads) are named as the historical backbone of the traffic.

Chapter 1 — Britain’s First Opium Wars

“From 1715, when the British East India Company opened up its first Far East office in the Chinese port city of Canton, it has been official British Crown policy to foster mass-scale drug addiction against targeted foreign populations…” The chapter traces East India Company opium from Bengal into China as a response to silver drain for tea and silk under King George III; links Adam Smith’s Wealth of Nations and Shelburne/Baring secret-committee machinery to both the American Revolution and the China trade; and presents the Opium Wars as enforced addiction for political control and looting rights. The chapter situates Matheson, Jardine, and related houses as the institutional core of the later “Dope, Inc.” system.

Chapter 2 — Palmerston’s Fifth Column, USA

Opening on “The assassination bureau,” the chapter argues that nineteenth-century narcotics traffic was already organized crime, run by Palmerston behind a screen of respectability and through his role as Patriarch of the Scottish Rite of Freemasonry. Palmerston’s “irregulars” in dope trafficking, assassinations, and Civil War–era fifth-column work are called the linear ancestors of modern organized crime. The model is the ethnic secret society—Chinese Triads, Order of Zion / B’nai B’rith, Young Italy / Mafia, Jesuits, Bakuninist anarchists—on a chain of command to Palmerston. American counterintelligence figures such as Poe and Morse are cited as contemporaries who understood the problem; Lincoln’s assassination is treated as part of the same web.

Chapter 3 — Britain’s “Noble Experiment”

Two “strategically important” events of 1918–1920: founding of the Royal Institute of International Affairs, and Prohibition. RIIA is traced to Cecil Rhodes’s will, the Round Table, Kipling, Milner, and “Milner’s Kindergarten” as a recruitment and planning agency for a “one world empire” that would reincorporate the United States. “On January 6 of the next year, Britain declared its opium war against the United States. Americans knew it as Prohibition.” Illegal alcohol and narcotics are presented as two product lines of one multinational firm, with British distillers, Far East refineries, and Canadian/Caribbean banks as suppliers and financiers. Canada goes wet as the U.S. goes dry; Britain fights League of Nations pressure to maintain Far Eastern opium production as heroin is outlawed in America in 1924.

Part II — How the Drug Empire Works

Introduction (Part II)

“The basis of this investigation.” The authors promise a guided tour from Golden Triangle mountains through Bangkok, Rangoon, and Singapore wholesalers; British trading-company logistics; Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation and other British banks; Pacific ports of entry; Canadian banks and “Zionist financiers”; and finally contact points in organized crime—so as to reconstruct the “Annual Report of Dope, Incorporated” (balance sheet, board, officers, subsidiaries). Illegal drugs are called the world’s biggest business “since the days of opium-pusher Adam Smith.”

Chapter 1 — Banking and the World’s Biggest Business

Hard evidence from DEA and other agencies, the authors say, allows only one conclusion: the drug industry is a single integrated world operation from poppy to street bag, better controlled than De Beers’ diamond CSO. The drug-related illegal economy is pegged at about $200 billion a year net. The problem is “purloined letter” obviousness: the traffic hides in plain sight because the British monarchy “organized most of the Far East to conform to the drug traffic” and because black money moves through the international banking system.

Chapter 2 — From Opium to Dirty Money

Price pyramid of Far Eastern opium/heroin: roughly 100/lbrawgumintheGoldenTrianglehighlands;about200/lb at triborder collection; division of a ~700-ton crop into heroin refining versus regional smoking opium. Wholesalers in Bangkok, Rangoon, or Hong Kong are treated as one business structure under old British banks and trading companies plus expatriate Chinese networks. The chapter is the bridge from agricultural production to financial laundering.

Chapter 3 — How the Drug Trade is Financed

Financial control “begins in Hong Kong” with Hong Kong dollar loans to expatriate Chinese operators, plus smuggler-sized gold bars, diamonds, and warehousing dominated by HongShang-related entities. Hongkong and Shanghai Bank is described as semi-official central bank of the Crown Colony, holder of a huge share of local banking, working closely with the colonial government, and the “financial hydra” unifying Asian opium production, transport, and distribution. Opacity of Hong Kong banking statistics is stressed.

Chapter 4 — Britain’s Gold and Dirty Diamond Operations

Gold and diamonds as untraceable media of the Asian drug trade: hundreds of tons of gold through the Orient yearly, mainly via Hong Kong and HongShang subsidiaries. Golden Triangle peasants are paid in goods and gold, not Bahamas accounts; the PRC’s share is said to be paid largely in gold across the Burmese border. Diamonds enter as portable, untraceable settlement. The gold trail is presented as a decisive clue that led investigators toward HongShang management.

Chapter 5 — Hong Kong: The World’s Drug Capital

In Hong Kong, drugs “do not merely dominate the economy: they are the economy.” Official addict estimates of 10 percent of population are raised by the authors toward a “safe, conservative” 20 percent (~1 million), generating multi-billion retail cash flow “at HongShang’s back porch.” Dirty money—retail dope, police bribes, wholesaling, gambling—is treated as the organizing principle of colonial economic life; comparison is made to New York’s addict population and dollar outflows.

Chapter 6 — The Peking Connection

Opens with Chou En-lai to Nasser (June 1965) on helping U.S. troops try opium and fighting the West “with their own weapons.” The chapter surveys pre-Kissinger Western and Soviet insistence on PRC opium production, then argues that Peking’s real money is in wholesaling, retailing, and financing through Hong Kong, not only farm-gate export (an ~$800 million primary-export figure is given as secondary). Since the Sino-Soviet split, Peking is said to have integrated external finance with British Hong Kong drug firms and expatriate Chinese networks as strategic policy.

Chapter 7 — How the Royal Institute of International Affairs Runs Drugs and Dirty Money

Command is traced “to Chatham House, St. James Square, London.” Far East traffic is a “British-Chinese joint venture” with Britain the senior partner, impossible without political approval, offshore credit markets, gold/diamonds, and management of retail crime. HongShang’s London Committee is treated as a special operation of top British banks. The chapter maps interlocking directorships from RIIA and related councils into drug, mining, shipping, Canadian, and “Zionist Lobby” dirty-money structures—the “Board of Directors of Drugs, Incorporated.”

Chapter 8 — Canada: North America’s Hong Kong

“Most heroin entering North America comes through Canada,” per the authors’ law-enforcement sources. Canada is denied nationhood in the American sense and described as run by the monarchy through Governor-General, Privy Council, and Knights of St. John who control business. Role is analogized to Prohibition-era transshipment. Official Secrets Act is blamed for silencing internal resistance; a minority of Canadian officials looking to the U.S. model are praised for fighting criminal activity that enjoys near-official sanction.

Chapter 9 — All in the Family: The Real Syndicate

Why has the picture stayed hidden? Beyond corporate facade, “the entire world drug traffic has been run by a single Family since its inception”—British oligarchic kinship, not only Italian, Jewish, or Chinese crime families. Keswicks of Jardine Matheson, Inchcapes of P&O, Russells, Dukes of Sutherland and Elgin reappear from the first Opium War to modern bank boards (HongShang, Jardine Matheson, Barclays, Anglo-American, N.M. Rothschild, Lazard). Ethnic underworld partners are called fitting allies of that oligarchy. A long “family tree” / dynasty chart apparatus (including Bruce and related lines in the illustration list) supports the claim that political, intelligence, and ideological support as well as growing and distribution sit inside one kinship system.

Part III — Organized Crime

Introduction (Part III)

Having covered production and wholesaling, the authors turn to distribution: Canadian board rooms where Keswicks and Inchcapes meet “Zionist Hofjuden middlemen,” above all the Bronfmans, for the U.S. market. Beneath Seagram respectability they promise to uncover control of the North American underworld; to treat Meyer Lansky, Marcello, and other “Mafia” figures as a British fifth column like China’s Green Gang; to follow money through casinos into legislatures and the “Zionist Lobby” (naming Edward Kennedy, Jacob Javits, Henry Jackson among others); and to track laundering through Basel, Liechtenstein, Tel Aviv, and the Bahamas into terrorism and a privately owned worldwide assassination bureau.

Chapter 1 — The Bronfman Gang

The Bronfmans are introduced via Seagram and public philanthropy, then as “the biggest bootleggers in North America” known a half-century earlier as “the Bronfman gang.” Yechiel (Ekiel) Bronfman’s 1889 arrival under Moses Montefiore Jewish Colonization auspices is tied to Hirsch/Rothschild colonization and Montreal Zionist elite clubs. The family path runs wood, horses, hotels/prostitution, then liquor; the Yiddish meaning “liquorman” is noted. Bootlegging, Canadian distilling, and later “respectable” corporate and Israeli philanthropic roles are presented as continuous with Hofjuden sponsorship and U.S. syndicate wholesale middleman functions.

Chapter 2 — The Kennedys: Organized Crime in the Government

The chapter asks why the Kennedys stay “clean” despite Chappaquiddick, Judith Exner/Giancana stories, and Joe Kennedy’s bootlegging reputation. Answer: from “Papa Joe” they were polished by the British as the respectable front for organized crime; “The Kennedy machine is organized crime in government.” Ted Kennedy’s 1976 Senate contributors are led by Joseph Linsey—linked to Lansky, International Airport Hotel Systems, Resorts International, and Raymond Patriarca—plus liquor and dog-track businesses. The chapter treats the Kennedy political machine as structurally mob-dependent regardless of any individual policy clash with Britain (as attributed to JFK in 1963).

Chapter 3 — Britain’s Assassination Bureau: Permindex

After JFK’s murder, New Orleans DA James Garrison’s trail leads to Montreal-based Permindex under Bronfman attorney Major Louis Mortimer Bloomfield; director Clay Shaw of the International Trade Mart is Garrison’s chief suspect, tied to Oswald and alleged escape channels. James Earl Ray’s Trade Mart visits and Canada–London flight after King’s murder are added. French SDECE is said to have exposed Permindex financing OAS attempts on de Gaulle via Geneva/Basel Zionist charities and Israeli fronts such as Bank Hapoalim. Mysterious deaths of witnesses and a smear campaign against Garrison end the New Orleans probe.

Chapter 4 — Permindex Unveiled: Resorts International / Intertel

After the 1974 collapse of Banque de Crédit Internationale (involving Evelyn de Rothschild and others in the authors’ account), Permindex capability is said not to die but to be upgraded at Paradise Island, Bahamas, as Resorts International and its security arm Intertel—with Meyer Lansky as éminence grise. Lansky’s “Hong Kong West” scheme for Caribbean gambling, narcotics, dirty money, and Murder Inc. is dated from about 1960 (Lucayan Beach Hotel via Louis Chesler, etc.). Resorts/Intertel become the near-shore headquarters of Bloomfield’s “Royal Commission of High Executioners” and dope billions.

Chapter 5 — The Jacobs Family’s Emprise: Sports and Crime

Dying reporter Don Bolles’s last words—“Mafia… Emprise… Adamson”—frame the chapter. Emprise (renamed Sportsystems in 1972), the Jacobs family’s conglomerate of hundreds of companies in racing, concessions, and sports, is called a leading quasi-legitimate laundry for narcotics, prostitution, and gambling, with major U.S., Canadian, and British holdings (including Letheby and Christopher Ltd. in England). Bolles’s Arizona investigation of dog and horse tracks is said never to have properly targeted Emprise despite his dying identification.

Chapter 6 — The Philadelphia Story

Old Philadelphia Quaker families and Britain’s Japhet opium line converge on Paco Pharmaceuticals in the Sun Oil building—packager of roughly two-thirds of U.S. amphetamine tablets for Smith Kline & French, Rohm and Haas, Pennwalt, and owned by Charterhouse Japhet. Paco is named as the main source of amphetamine “leakage” into illicit channels. The chapter inventories DEA/BNDD/FDA actions against Pennwalt and related firms (fines, Mexican subsidiary discrepancies, opium warehouse “leakage,” Sixty Minutes coverage) as a case history from wholesale production “down to the gutter.”

Part IV — Creating the Market: The British Origins of the Counterculture

A continuous essay section (no numbered chapter list in the contents). It opens with Paul Ghalioungui on ancient Egyptian Isis-cult rites using hashish or mescal for trance, then asserts that the 1960s drug-rock counterculture is a literal resurrection of that cult (including the Isis cross as popular symbol). The counterculture is framed as thirty years of British Opium War against the United States—chemical and psychological warfare plus Vietnam. Oligarchies foster superstition, magic, hallucinogens, and “if it feels good, do it” to keep populations incapable of acting in their own interests.

“The high priesthood”: Aldous Huxley is named “case officer,” grandson of T.H. Huxley (Round Table) and lifelong collaborator of Arnold Toynbee (RIIA council, wartime British intelligence research, Churchill briefing). Toynbee’s imperial-decline theory is said to require a trained priesthood of imperial rule; Huxley’s “Children of the Sun” circle (Eliot, Auden, Mosley, D.H. Lawrence, etc.) prepares the ground. LSD introduction is attributed to Huxley as “designated Crown agent,” with RIIA and Tavistock as institutional psychological-warfare arms. Rock music, campus revolt, and mass drug use are treated as market-creation for Dope, Inc., not spontaneous youth culture.

Part V — The Drug Lobby: The Criminals Come Out in the Open

“Britain’s secret army of organized crime is now out in the open demanding the expansion of its dope market.” California decriminalization bills (sponsor Willie Brown, called a Resorts International lobbyist), post-1973 successes decriminalizing marijuana in nine states, and DEA director Peter Bensinger on Maine as a smuggling gateway are cited. Peter Bourne (Carter drug advisor) is attacked for televised cocaine-legalization advocacy. State and federal pushes to reclassify heroin for medical/“experimental” use (Ohio, New Mexico, Florida; historical New York 1919 parallel) are described as the next step after marijuana. Congressional vehicles named include the Javits–Koch marijuana bill and Kennedy-authored S.1437 overhauling federal criminal codes on a British common-law basis. The Most Venerable Order of St. John of Jerusalem is said to run the U.S. drug lobby “out front.”

Epilogue

On 18 October 1978 the authors met Federal Reserve officials (including John E. Ryan and Robert Mannion) and HongShang counsel (including Steuart L. Pittman) to argue against HongShang’s proposed acquisition of 51 percent of Marine Midland; a similar meeting followed with the New York State Banking Authority. Manuscript excerpts had circulated to State, Treasury, DEA, and related agencies; public meetings and broadcasts spread the thesis. The epilogue answers “How will the powerful men who stand behind the narcotics traffic react?” with the 11 November Paris meeting attacked by about twenty hooded Betar militants (one policeman injured)—an assault French authorities, the authors say, linked to Mossad, with Betar’s Jabotinsky–Begin lineage noted.
