The People’s Peace: Britain Since 1945 (Morgan, 4th ed. 2021)

A single-author narrative political history of Britain from 1945 to 2020, tracing the post-war settlement, consensus and its undoing, Thatcherism, New Labour, and the years from financial crash to Brexit.

Summary

Kenneth O. Morgan’s The People’s Peace is a continuous narrative of British politics, society, and external policy from the end of the Second World War through 2020. First published in 1990, it appeared as an OUP paperback with corrections in 1992; a second edition (1999) carried the story past Major; a third edition was issued as Britain Since 1945 (2001); the fourth and revised edition (2021) adds Part IV on the new millennium through crash and Brexit. Copyright is stated as © Kenneth O. Morgan 1990, 1999, 2001, 2021.

The fourth edition is organised in four parts and sixteen chapters: I. The Era of Advance, 1945–1961 (chs 1–5); II. The Years of Retreat, 1961–1979 (chs 6–11); III. Thatcherism and its Aftermath, 1979–2001 (chs 12–14); IV. The New Millennium, 2001–2020 (chs 15–16). Apparatus includes prefaces to later editions, list of plates, select bibliography (manuscript collections and printed sources), and index. Chapter summaries below follow body openers in the local epub extract.

Chapters

Part I — The Era of Advance, 1945–1961

Chapter 1 — The Façade of Unity

“‘National unity’, declared Sir William Beveridge in 1943, was the great moral achievement of the Second World War.” Morgan opens by setting post-1945 interpretations—decline and eclipse versus wartime solidarity—against wartime images of common citizenship and social rights from the 1942 Beveridge Report. He contrasts later Thatcherite readings of war as missed free-market opportunities, left critiques that stressed how little class structure changed after 1945, and technocratic diagnoses. Churchill’s limited traditional unity, Bevin’s corporatism, Keynesian and planning ethics, Mass Observation and social policy are weighed against evidence that war also hardened distinctions. The chapter frames the ambivalent legacy that Labour after 1945 claimed as national mandate.

Chapter 2 — Labour’s High Noon, 1945–1947

“‘The Socialist advance continues’, proclaimed the Labour Chancellor of the Exchequer, Hugh Dalton, in May 1946.” Morgan describes an exciting time for the policy elite amid drab shortages and bread rationing for the mass public. Nationalisation proceeds largely through Morrison’s public-board model with limited controversy; the welfare state is widely accepted; the National Health Service is far more contested. Bevan’s NHS negotiations, housing difficulties, limits of educational advance, regional relocation of industry under Cripps, and more uncertain external affairs—including imperial retreat and the loss of India—define the high noon of Attlee’s first years.

Chapter 3 — The Collectivist Retreat, 1948–1951

After the fuel and convertibility crises of 1947, government and nation move into a culture of “consolidation.” Cripps becomes the public giant of austerity, devaluation (1949), and wage restraint; Morrison’s Festival of Britain idea offers cheer in drab years. Full employment, modest leisure and mass sport, healthier school medical statistics, and class-polarised culture (including middle-class grievance) set the social scene. The February 1950 election underlines regional and class gulfs; external dependence on the United States deepens; Britain’s world role remains claimed even as economic autonomy shrinks, carrying the narrative to Labour’s 1951 defeat.

Chapter 4 — The Conservative Compromise, 1951–1956

Churchill’s return is widely expected as a sharp break; Morgan argues the expectation is misplaced. Balance-of-payments crisis, one-nation ministers (Eden, Butler, Macmillan), conciliation of unions, limited denationalisation, and defence of the NHS and social services define continuity. Macmillan’s housing drive and The Economist’s “Butskellism” label capture the consensus mood. Refusal to “set the pound free” prioritises social and Commonwealth stability over market radicalism; by mid-decade affluence, new towns, and cultural revival mark the Conservative compromise before Suez.

Chapter 5 — The Zenith of One-Nation Toryism, 1957–1961

“On 3 January 1957, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, Harold Macmillan, addressed his Cabinet colleagues on the lessons of Suez.” Morgan follows cut-backs in overseas obligations, Kenya and Cyprus as main military engagements, slow progress of the Central African Federation, and West African independence (Ghana 1957, Nigeria 1960, Sierra Leone 1961, Gambia 1963). Middle Eastern limits appear in 1958 crises; unilateral military action increasingly falls to the United States. The Bermuda Conference with Eisenhower confirms dependence within revived Anglo-American defence intimacy and residual mistrust. Macmillan’s approach to the new European Common Market is wary; at home, one-nation Toryism and affluence mark the zenith before Part II’s “retreat.”

Part II — The Years of Retreat, 1961–1979

Chapter 6 — The Stagnant Society, 1961–1964

“In the 1950s, Britain had offered the world, many felt, a spectacle of hedonism and insular complacency.” Discontent arrives largely from books—Michael Shanks’s The Stagnant Society, Penguin “What is Wrong with Britain” volumes, rediscovery of the working class in culture and social criticism, and scare of criminality and cultural dissolution. Macmillan’s government moves toward immigration restriction (1962); Welsh and Scottish grievances and Saunders Lewis’s Welsh-language campaign rise; Northern Ireland’s frozen Protestant ascendancy cannot escape the same pressures. Youth styles, Bishop of Woolwich’s Honest to God, satire, and Private Eye mark a less deferential age.

Chapter 7 — Labour Blown Off Course, 1964–1967

“Despite its narrow majority, Harold Wilson’s Labour government took office in October 1964 amidst a mood of some optimism.” Early glow covers arts (Jennie Lee), Robbins-era universities, and liberal social mood; underneath lies an £800 million balance-of-payments crisis and an unfavourable world reaction (“gone to Canossa”). Brown’s planning impulse yields to traditional Treasury recovery of parity; political structure mixes competence (Jenkins, Healey) with paranoia and survival politics after Gordon Walker’s electoral failures. Wilson remains master of party and Commons while economic and political indirection blow Labour off course.

Chapter 8 — Years of Hard Slog, 1968–1970

“In the aftermath of the devaluation of the pound, the Labour government of Harold Wilson entered upon a grim period of deflation and austerity.” Jenkins’s Treasury dominance imposes Korean War–style restrictions, regulator tax rises, and hire-purchase controls; by-election losses mount. Defence posture winds down east-of-Suez commitments even as residual Gulf presence continues. Domestic turbulence includes race (Powell’s 1968 speeches; nativist organisations), liberal Home Office counter-policies under Jenkins, and rising Scottish and Welsh nationalism (Gwynfor Evans in Parliament; Plaid Cymru growth), carrying the government to 1970 defeat.

Chapter 9 — The Heath Experiment, 1970–1974

“Later commentators have not been kind to the government of Edward Heath between June 1970 and March 1974.” Morgan stresses Heath’s distant, hard-working personality, absence of traditional grandees, and the swing from Selsdon free-market rhetoric to mid-term reverse: Barber’s early decontrol, then corporatism, industry intervention, and incomes policy. Miners’ strike, three-day week, and Industrial Relations Act failure dominate; public culture and the post-war social compact are weakened. The government exits in March 1974 after confrontation with the miners and unworkable industrial relations law.

Chapter 10 — Challenge to Consensus, 1974–1976

“‘Bleak House rather than Great Expectations’ was how a budget earlier in the century had been greeted. The mood was not dissimilar when Harold Wilson returned.” Minority government, price explosion and inflation, July pump-priming, and the October 1974 “short parliament” election frame self-preservation over innovation. Callaghan–Kissinger working relationship; renegotiation of EEC terms and the 1975 Common Market referendum (public funding, pro-market press, “foregone conclusion”) settle Europe only short-term. Celtic devolution after Kilbrandon is treated as another pragmatic half-answer; Scottish pressure (SNP seats) forces Labour to formulate a devolution approach to limit party defection.

Chapter 11 — The Years of Discontent, 1977–1979

“Britain after the IMF crisis had an air of being a nation after the deluge.” Callaghan is cast as a new Baldwin—cautious, adroit, not an institutional radical—presiding over recovery in 1977–78: falling inflation from a 25 per cent peak, improved balance of payments, and a temporary glow of good news. Unsolved legacies remain: Commonwealth decay, Gibraltar, Northern Ireland deadlock under Roy Mason, and Scottish and Welsh devolution bills whose public support softens. Industrial relations and wages policy unravel as the £6 flat-rate deal expires; hostility to union power and the “winter of discontent” frame the road to 1979.

Part III — Thatcherism and its Aftermath, 1979–2001

Chapter 12 — The Foundations of Thatcherism, 1979–1983

“More than any change of government since 1945, Margaret Thatcher’s election victory was taken as marking a decisive shift in the national mood.” Morgan stresses personality and conviction politics over systematic philosophy; early radicalism is slower than rhetoric because she is not yet mistress of her Cabinet. Howe’s June 1979 budget sets monetarist markers; high interest rates and recession force pragmatic adjustment from 1982. Prior’s cautious union law, limited early privatisation, council-house sales, Zimbabwe settlement, and an initially simple foreign-policy stance show foundations rather than full New Right transformation—before the Falklands and 1983 majority deepen the project.

Chapter 13 — High Noon For the New Right—Resurgence or Retreat?, 1983–1990

Second-term radicalism: large-scale privatisation, Lawson’s tax-cutting budgets (standard rate to 27p in 1987), Tebbit-era union legislation, and the 1984–85 miners’ strike as supreme test—ending in union defeat without power cuts. Assaults on local government (abolition of metropolitan authorities), quangos, Arts Council patronage patterns, press constraints, Church tensions, and universities illustrate centralisation paradoxically pursued by an anti-statist government. Westland exposes dictatorial tendencies; personalised Cabinet power and later poll-tax crisis prepare Thatcher’s fall.

Chapter 14 — Fin de Siècle: New Labour in Power, 1990–2001

Thatcher’s fall is a defining public moment; Major’s likeable style does not cure Conservative unpopularity, yet 1992 still returns a Conservative majority and appears to confirm Tory England. “Black Wednesday” (16 September 1992) shatters ERM policy and economic reputation. Europe and Maastricht torment the party; sleaze and policy drift deepen malaise. The chapter carries the narrative through Major’s decline to Blair’s 1997 victory and the first New Labour years through 2001—rebranding, constitutional reform momentum, and the fin-de-siècle resettlement of British politics after eighteen years of Conservative rule.

Part IV — The New Millennium, 2001–2020

Chapter 15 — Millennial Perspectives, 2001–2008

“The British entered the new millennium in sober and unheroic mood.” Finance and credit dominate a once-manufacturing economy; Old Labour tools seem obsolete. Blair’s personal charisma and triangulation structure politics; public heroes are drawn from the past (Churchill) more than the present. Social peace coexists with stubborn poverty (Rowntree) and sharp class division; gay equality advances as church influence wanes; multiculturalism and post-2004 EU migration reshape debate (Parekh report; “chicken tikka masala” as national dish rhetoric). Devolution beds in for Scotland and Wales while England remains the constitutional “black hole.” Science (genetics) and cultural confidence contrast with unheroic politics through Brown’s succession on the eve of crash.

Chapter 16 — From Crash to Brexit, 2008–2020

“Towards the end of the first decade of the twenty-first century, the course of British history alarmingly changed.” Brown faces collapsing receipts, unemployment, and banking crisis after the U.S. sub-prime shock; 2009 is spent clearing wreckage. The May 2010 election produces the first hung parliament since 1929 and a Conservative–Liberal Democrat Coalition. Austerity, welfare caps, and rising inequality follow; Lib Dems are damaged by association. The Scottish independence referendum and SNP sweep in 2015 mark a landmark for the Union. Thereafter politics is “convulsed by intense and bitter argument on Europe”: UKIP’s leverage, Cameron’s referendum pledge, Cabinet splits, and the path through the 2016 Leave vote and its aftermath to 2020 close the book’s narrative.
