Secret Affairs: Britain’s Collusion with Radical Islam (Curtis)

A research monograph, grounded in declassified files and open sources, arguing that postwar British foreign policy has systematically colluded with radical Islamic states and movements—Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, the Muslim Brotherhood milieu, and assorted jihadist networks—to serve imperial residue, Cold War, and financial-order objectives, at lasting cost to British security.

Summary

Mark Curtis’s Secret Affairs (new updated edition 2018; first edition 2010) fills what he calls a missing link in commentary on British terrorism and Middle East policy: Whitehall’s long record of collusion with radical Islam. After 7/7 and later wars in Libya and Syria, public debate stresses home-grown radicalisation or blowback from Iraq and Afghanistan; Curtis adds a deeper pattern of alliance with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan and with extremist movements and, at times, outright jihadist groups. Both Saudi Arabia and Pakistan are described as partly British creations; Islamist forces have been useful as counterweights to nationalism and communism, as covert assets, and as props of a pro-Western financial order (Saudi investment in London). The book rests partly on National Archives research into declassified British files, plus leaks, admissions, and secondary reporting. Twenty-two chapters run from imperial divide-and-rule through partition, Iran and Egypt, the Afghan jihad, al-Qaida, Bosnia, “Londonistan,” 7/7, Iraq and Afghanistan, the Arab Spring, Syria and Islamic State, to UK terror attacks and the “Whitehall connection.”

Chapters

Introduction

During Middle East wars and upheavals, one aspect of British policy is little mentioned: collusion with radical Islam. The July 2005 London bombings killed 52 people; the threat persists (Scotland Yard warnings into late 2017). Curtis rejects accounts that stop at home-grown radicalisation or military interventions alone. Britain has been in permanent strategic alliance with some radical Islamic forces for long-term goals, and has worked covertly with jihadist groups. Two linked sets of actors: (1) states—especially Saudi Arabia and Pakistan (Zia ul-Haq’s sponsorship of terrorist groups after the coup)—and (2) extremist movements and organisations. Roots go to imperial divide-and-rule; after 1945, planners faced loss of empire and two superpowers; as forces withdrew from the Middle East in the late 1960s, Saudi and other Islamist forces filled space; the 1980s Afghan war spawned al-Qaida-linked networks; London often gave refuge or asylum while extremists continued overseas activity. Five uses of radical Islamic forces are listed; two geo-strategic objectives—regional influence and Britain’s place in a pro-Western financial order—are emphasised. Curtis denies that jihadism is a pure British “creation,” citing Olivier Roy’s definition of Islamic radicalism, and describes months of National Archives research.

Chapter 1 — Imperial Divide and Rule

Roots of postwar collaboration lie in empire. Britain often fought Muslim powers and jihadist movements and feared pan-Islam, yet also ruled through Muslim elites: Great Game props for decaying Muslim states against Russia; Princely States and communal textbook policies in India to strengthen community differences; explicit preference for communal division as a “strong point” of British political position. Muslim revivalism challenged the Raj even as divide-and-rule instrumentalised Islam.

Chapter 2 — Partition in India and Palestine

End-of-empire partition settlements create lasting communal and geopolitical structures—Pakistan as an Islamic state and the Palestine settlement—within which later British Islamist collusion unfolds.

Chapter 3 — Shock Troops in Iran and Egypt

Early Cold War use of Islamist and clerical networks as “shock troops” against nationalism in Iran and Egypt, continuing imperial techniques under new strategic labels.

Chapter 4 — Islam versus Nationalism

Systematic preference for Islamic political forces over secular nationalist projects across the Middle East, as British power contracts and U.S. primacy rises.

Chapter 5 — The Global Islamic Mission

Saudi and related global missionary (da‘wa) infrastructures as instruments that expand conservative Islam internationally with Western acquiescence or support.

Chapter 6 — ‘Handy Weapons’ in Jordan and Egypt

Islamist groups treated as disposable or deniable assets in Jordanian and Egyptian politics—useful against leftists and nationalists, dangerous when uncontrolled.

Chapter 7 — The Saudi and Iranian Revolutions

Contrasting trajectories: the Saudi system as a long-term British (and then Anglo-American) partner versus the Iranian revolution as the Islamist force that escaped Western management.

Chapter 8 — Training in Terrorism: The Afghan Jihad

British role, alongside the United States and Pakistan, in the 1980s Afghan jihad: training, facilitation, and the seeding of transnational terrorist capacity.

Chapter 9 — The Dictator, the King and the Ayatollah

Triangular Middle East politics among secular dictators, Saudi monarchy, and revolutionary Iran; British choices among them.

Chapter 10 — Nurturing al-Qaida

How networks that became al-Qaida were tolerated, used, or inadequately constrained within the broader jihad infrastructure Britain helped sustain.

Chapter 11 — Pakistan’s Surge into Central Asia

Pakistan’s post-Afghan forward policy into Central Asia and its implications for British and Western security partnerships with Islamabad.

Chapter 12 — A Covert War in Bosnia

British and Western collusion or facilitation involving Islamist fighters in the Bosnian war.

Chapter 13 — Killing Qadafi, Overthrowing Saddam

Libya and Iraq policies as continuations of regime-change strategies that again intersected with Islamist forces and regional sponsors.

Chapter 14 — Intrigues in the Southern Balkans

Further Balkan covert politics involving Islamist networks after Bosnia.

Chapter 15 — 9/11 Connections

British dimensions of the background to the September 11 attacks—relationships, refuges, and intelligence failures or choices.

Chapter 16 — Londonistan: A ‘Green Light’ to Terrorism

London as a permissive environment for extremist organising and propaganda—“Londonistan”—alleged green lights or wilful blindness serving foreign-policy equities.

Chapter 17 — 7/7 and the London–Islamabad Axis

The July 2005 bombings read through the lens of the Britain–Pakistan intelligence and militant nexus rather than solely as isolated home-grown crime.

Chapter 18 — Alliances of Moderation

Official language of “moderate” Muslim partners as cover for continued relationships with problematic regimes and movements.

Chapter 19 — Allied to the Enemy: Iraq and Afghanistan

Post-9/11 wars in which Britain is formally at war with forces its past policies helped empower, while still allied to state sponsors with dual policies.

Chapter 20 — Good and Bad Revolutions: The Arab Spring

Selective British responses to 2011 uprisings—distinguishing “good” and “bad” revolutions according to strategic interest, including Islamist winners.

Chapter 21 — Islamic State, al-Qaida and Covert Action in Syria

Syria’s war: covert action, rebel sponsorship, and the rise of Islamic State and al-Qaida affiliates within a field Britain helped shape.

Chapter 22 — UK Terror Attacks: The Whitehall Connection

Domestic UK terror attacks linked back to the historical pattern of Whitehall collusion, refuge, and dual policy toward radical Islam.
